Map console — 16 notes

How to use this map

These two are the shortlist itself, drawn geographically. Parcels are everything passing the filters you set on the left; the Site Screener results layer is the ranked shortlist from the screener. Seeing them on a map answers what a table cannot: whether your best sites cluster, and how they sit against transmission, water and county posture. A shortlist that scatters across eight counties is a different plan from one that concentrates in two.
Rail and highway access decide how construction loads and transformers reach the site. Declared surplus is the cleanest owner-motivation signal we hold: every other one infers willingness from distress, while a Report of Excess is an owner publicly stating in a federal filing that it does not want the property. There are only 20, and that is the honest number — the other 1,533 federal points are in active use and are context, not leads. A cancelled grid project is the same idea from a different direction: the landowner already signed an interconnection agreement, so consent and a studied grid position exist and the project does not. Colleges are where operations staff come from; USDA-inspected food plants are heavy industrial sites that already carry large power, large water and a discharge permit.
These measure distance to a wire, which is not the same as capacity to spare. A parcel beside a full substation is worse than one three miles from a bus with headroom. For capacity, use the Site screener.
Substations, lines and buses are three different answers, and only one of them is about capacity. A line tells you power passes nearby; a substation tells you it is transformed nearby; a bus is the only one of the three that carries a number for how much you could actually take. A parcel beside a full substation is a worse site than one three miles from a bus with headroom — so use distance to decide construction cost, and the bus to decide whether the project is possible at all. See the Power & grid page for every bus in both directions.
This is the future, and everything else here is the present. A substation being rebuilt to a higher voltage in 2027 changes what a site beside it can ask for in 2028 — and a utility that has already committed capital near your parcel is a very different negotiation from one that has not. ⚠ Only 119 of 618 planned items name a station we can place. The other 499 name only a utility; they are shown against that utility’s service territory on the grid page rather than invented into a point here.
The queue is your competition for the same study slots and the same headroom. Every point here is a project that got there first; a cluster of them around a bus is a warning that the number you are reading may already be spoken for by the time your study runs.
This decides who you negotiate with, and it is the first phone call you make. The territory owning your parcel sets the tariff you pay, runs the load study, and determines whether the state commission is involved at all — an investor-owned utility answers to the IURC, a municipal or a REMC largely does not. ⚠ A parcel straddling two territories is reported as straddling rather than assigned to one, because guessing which one serves it would decide your rate for you.
Voltage class is the fastest read on the map — a 765 kV backbone and a sub-100 kV lateral look the same on a plain map and are not remotely the same siting fact. Unknown voltage has its own color and is never drawn as low voltage. Gas matters if you intend on-site generation; a 300 MW plant needs roughly 50 MMcf/d. Large emitters signal neighbours who already hold air permits, so the permitting path is precedented rather than novel.
Public records — unpaid taxes, a foreclosure, a demolition order — suggesting an owner may transact. Non-residential only, and only trouble serious enough to plausibly move someone to sell.
Undated is not old. 90% of our largest signal (unpaid taxes) carries no date, so a bare “last 90 days” cut would delete nine tenths of it for a reason about our collection, not about the property. They are kept by default; untick to exclude them.
The first three are hard constraints that can stop a project. The last is an upside — areas where a coal plant or mine closed carry a larger federal tax credit.
Five of these six are money and the sixth can stop the project. An energy community or low-income designation adds up to 10 percentage points to the investment tax credit, which on a capital-heavy build is the largest single swing on this whole console — worth chasing deliberately rather than discovering later. Critical habitat is the opposite: a federal consultation that can add a year or end the siting outright. They were one checkbox until 2026-08-19, which meant a developer chasing the adder could not separate it from the blocker.

⚠ THREE OF THESE LOOK ALIKE AND ONLY ONE IS YOUR CREDIT. All three are low-income tract designations, drawn by three different agencies for three different purposes:
• Energy community — the IRA designation (Treasury/IRS). A closed coal mine or plant, or a fossil-employment tract meeting an unemployment test. This is the one that pays you: up to 10 percentage points on the investment tax credit for the project itself.
• Opportunity zone — a 2017 Act designation (Treasury/IRS), nominated by the governor. The benefit belongs to an INVESTOR, not the project: capital-gains deferral and reduction for someone rolling a realised gain into a qualified fund. It changes who can cheaply finance you, not what you can claim.
• Qualified census tract — a HUD designation (≥50% of households under 60% of area median income, or a poverty rate ≥25%). It exists to allocate the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit. It gives an energy project nothing at all — it is shown because it is a useful read on the community you would be building in, and because a reader who sees it beside the other two would otherwise assume it is money.
A 100 MW evaporative-cooled campus consumes roughly 1–2 million gallons a day of make-up water, so the question is not whether water is nearby but whether a body is large enough to permit an intake against. Only named reaches over a kilometer and bodies over 12 acres are drawn — farm ponds are left out deliberately, because drawing them would answer the question wrongly in the reassuring direction. Pair it with the county water panel, which gives the county’s entire public-supply withdrawal in the same units.
These four say who else already holds a say over the land, which is not something zoning or the environmental gates will tell you. A large load near an active installation can draw a DoD Siting Clearinghouse review that runs on the Department’s clock, not the county’s — it rarely refuses, it adds months. Tribal trust land is a different sovereign entirely, so the counterparty is the tribal government rather than the county. Special-use airspace governs what may be built tall — cooling towers, stacks and cranes, not the slab. And every obstruction drawn here is at or above the 200 ft FAA notice threshold, so a cluster of them is cheap evidence that a tall structure of your own will clear the same review. Green obstructions are wind turbines, and those are a signal rather than a constraint: 1,816 of the 4,590 are turbines, and a standing turbine is proof that this exact ground already cleared landowner consent, an interconnection and a local permit — three of the four things a siter has to establish from scratch anywhere else.
A watershed is the unit water is actually allocated and argued over in — two parcels a mile apart in different subbasins can face different objections and different permitting bodies. Click one to see the rivers, reservoirs and lakes it holds. Power generation is already Indiana’s largest water user (3,822 of 7,177 Mgal/d) and 99.5% of that is surface water, so a new thermal load competes for exactly what these layers show.
County-level, not parcel-level. Opposition intensity partly tracks news volume, so large metros read higher for reasons that are not posture. Absence of a recorded rule is not evidence there is no rule — Indiana's data-center regulation is mostly county moratoria that no code library carries.
Existing data centers are both competition for the same power and evidence a county has already accepted one. ⚠ An approval never cancels a ban: where a county has both, the restriction still governs.

Seller intent — 3 notes

Which owners might sell you land — and how strongly we believe it

This is the question the page exists to answer. Every parcel below is non-residential and carries at least one admitted owner-motivation signal. They are ranked by how many different kinds of distress stack up on one property, because a single signal can be noise while a demolition order sitting on top of an unsafe building on top of repeated violations is a story you can open a conversation with. Read the why column, then take the parcel to the map console for its grid and land position.
Recency does not mean the same thing for every signal. A foreclosure goes stale; contamination does not heal because the record is old. A parcel whose evidence is all old is marked as such rather than dropped.

Every severity filter we apply, named — and what each one removes

A severity threshold decides which parcels you see, and you cannot audit what you cannot see. There are exactly three, plus the standing non-residential rule. Each one is a judgement we made, so each is stated with the vocabulary it acts on, the count it removed, and the reason a surviving row plausibly points at a willing seller. ⛔ Nothing is deleted — excluded rows keep an admit_status and stay queryable.

⚠ Does an environmental violation really mean someone will sell?

Operator, 2026-08-19: “an environmental violation usually doesn’t result in a sale, so why do we believe our environmental violations will?” Taken as a live challenge to the signal, and it only half survives.

Community & posture — 9 notes

Receipts browser — every item, newest first

This is the evidence behind every county color on the map. Before you trust a posture, read what produced it — a single news article and an adopted ordinance are not the same weight, and the difference decides whether you walk away or send a lawyer. When you do file, this is where the specific docket number or ordinance to cite comes from. Audit trail, not a site test.

County posture (from held receipts)

The county-level go / think-hard / no-go read, and the fastest way to cut a target list before you look at a single parcel.

County data-center actions — the layer no code library carries

This is the only thing on any page that can stop a project outright, and it is measured in council calendar — months, not design weeks. Everything else in this application changes cost or schedule; a ban changes whether the site exists for you. Read the expiry where one is shown: a moratorium with a date is a timing problem, while a ban with none is a siting decision.

Local data-center ordinances — what is codified, and what is not

Codified and uncodified are different risks, not different wordings. A codified ordinance is a known quantity: you can read the setbacks, noise limits and district table and design to them. An uncodified moratorium or resolution is a moving target that can be extended, tightened or replaced at a single meeting, so it needs a diary entry rather than a design response. ⚠ A district table with every cell blank means permitted nowhere — that is a prohibition written as silence.

Indiana legislature — energy & data-center bills (the P7 preview)

State law is the one thing that can move every county at once — it can pre-empt local restrictions, or hand counties new powers to impose them, and it sets the sales-tax treatment that changes a build’s economics outright. But a bill is not law. Use this to see what could change inside your development timeline, not as a fact about today’s rules — the county tables above are what governs right now.

Federal disaster declarations — Indiana history

Repeated federal declarations of the same hazard in a county are what an insurer and a structural engineer price. It shows up as premium, design standard and expected downtime — not as a reason to reject a site, but as a cost you should carry in the model rather than discover later. County-grain context.

Severe weather events by type (NOAA storm events)

The hazard mix matters more than the count. Tornado and hail frequency drive the structural and roof spec on a very large single-storey building, and wind events drive how much you spend hardening the interconnection you just paid for. Pair it with the flood and wetland gates on the map. County-grain context, not a site test.

Water stress in the basins Indiana sits in

A withdrawal permit is argued at BASIN level, not county level, so two parcels a few miles apart can face different objectors. Baseline water stress is the ratio of withdrawals to available supply — it governs how CONTESTED a new withdrawal will be, which is a different question from whether water is physically there.

Court activity by county — mortgage foreclosures & evictions (IOCS)

A read on local economic distress, which is what makes land loose and prices negotiable — useful for choosing which county to shop in. ⛔ It can never identify a building, and must not be read as one: the source counts cases per court, so a high number tells you about a county’s economy, never about the parcel you are looking at. The parcel-level version of this question is the Owner-motivation feed.

Yearly cost proxy — from published tariff structures (cross-check grade)

A first-pass power bill for your load, good enough to rank utilities and rule out obviously expensive service before you spend anything. Change the MW and load factor above and the ordering is the useful output — not the absolute figure.

Gas OAC — Indiana locations (Panhandle Eastern & Trunkline publish county)

This is the only gas number at a grain you can site against — a named delivery point, in a named county, with what the pipeline says is unsold today. The last column turns that into the generation it could actually support. Roughly 50 MMcf/d feeds a 300 MW plant, so a point posting a few thousand dekatherms a day is a boiler connection, not a data-center power supply.
What to do with it: shortlist points whose spare capacity clears your load with margin, then confirm with the pipeline — posted capacity is a daily operational figure, not a reservation, and it can be sold to someone else tomorrow. Texas Eastern and REX are BLOCKED (walls recorded), so a gap here is not an absence of gas.

Delivered fuel cost at Indiana plants ($/MMBtu)

This is the floor under your power price — what Indiana generators actually paid for fuel, before any margin, delivery or rider. The ¢/kWh column converts it at a combined-cycle heat rate so you can hold it against a retail tariff: a bundled quote sitting near this number is a modelling error, not a bargain. It also sets the economics of running your own generation.

Demand response — load the utilities can already shed

Two readings. It measures how much peak the utility can already remove without building anything, so a utility with deep demand response has more room for a new large load than its headline capacity suggests. It also signals whether an interruptible or curtailable tariff is likely to be offered to you — those trade a lower price for the utility's right to cut you, which suits a BESS far better than a data center carrying an uptime commitment. Context for the negotiation, not a site test.

FERC EQR filers with an Indiana contact address

These are counterparties, not sites — companies already selling wholesale power that list an Indiana contact address, which makes this a starting call-list for a PPA or a block purchase rather than anything about land. Quarters filed is a crude liveness check: a filer that stopped years ago is not a counterparty today. Context only.

The rate engine — what we can defend, and what we refuse to quote

Operator ruling, 2026-08-17: this is NOT a floor under an Indiana bundled rate, and it is no longer presented as one. The old framing was borrowed from a restructured market, where a retail supplier prices energy off the wholesale market and the utility bills regulated wires on top — there, wholesale really does flow through.
Indiana is vertically integrated. The utility owns its generation and recovers it through base rates set in an IURC rate case, so a bundled customer never buys at the market price. What actually passes fuel and purchased-power cost through is the Fuel Adjustment Clause, plus the RTO cost rider — and the utility’s embedded generation cost can sit either side of today’s market price, so a rate below LMP is not evidence of an error.
What it is still good for: the cost of energy if you buy it as energy — a PPA, a block purchase, or behind-the-meter generation — and as a read on regional energy cost. Treat it as an alternative procurement route, not as a bound on the tariff.
⚠ We do not hold a tariff book for this utility. What follows is URDB's flattened summary — the published energy and demand rates only.

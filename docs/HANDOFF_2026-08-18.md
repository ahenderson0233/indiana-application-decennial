## 8. THE RIDER STACK WAS NEVER COUNTED — on 17 of 18 IOU schedules

**Symptom:** the Riders column read **$0** for every schedule of every IOU except I&M IP-LL.

**Cause — ordering, not matching.** `service_class()` returns a component's raw `applies_to` as its
class KEY. A rider's `applies_to` is `hl (all voltages), pl` / `rate hlf` / `rate 632` — never a
service class — and the renderer's per-class filter is `c.volt === volt`, so **every rider was
dropped at every service voltage**. The schedule's own components are normalised in a loop that runs
**before** the riders are appended, so the riders never passed through it.

⭐ **Why it survived a full session of auditing:** `n_riders_attached` reported 8–13 riders
correctly attached the whole time, and the previous session's fix for I&M's punctuation defect was
verified against **that count**. The riders were selected. Their money was never counted. *A count
is not a total*, and the check that would have caught it — does the Riders column ever contain a
number? — is the one nobody wrote.

**What it moved, measured through the shipped renderer at 300 MW / 85% LF:**

|---|---|---|---|
| SIGECO **LP** (transmission delivery) | 5.81¢ **−36%** | 8.58¢ **−5%** | 9.02¢ |

| I&M **IP** (transmission) | 5.30¢ **−34%** | 6.89¢ **−14%** | 8.04¢ |

## 9. EIGHT MORE DEFECTS, FOUND BY WORKING THE LIST DOWN

Each was measured before and after. Full detail is in **BACKLOG G54**; the shapes worth carrying:

1. **A comma between two digits is a thousands separator, not a class separator.** Duke writes
   `transmission (138,000/230,000/345,000 V)`; the generic multi-class test read those digit-group
   commas as a class list, merged Duke's two **alternative** transmission rates and **summed** them
   — 23.59 + 20.51 $/kW-month and 0.046775 + 0.044002 $/kWh. **Duke HLF: +92% → −4%/+5%.**
2. **Never infer a service class from a charge NAME.** NIPSCO 631 has a *"Transmission charge"* —
   cost recovery at every voltage — and reading the word out of its name gave it the transmission
   family, collided it with an unrelated row, made `transmission` look like a family NIPSCO splits,
   and **dropped $0.014689/kWh: $32.8M a year**. Measured across all five books, removing the name
   fallback changes **exactly 2 components**, and both are the 631 rows.
3. **Optional services were billed as firm.** NIPSCO puts maintenance service at **$0.62/kW-DAY**
   inside its schedules — unavailable June–September, capped at 60 days a year — and we charged it
   365 days: **+$106.22M/yr on Rate 632 alone**. Also back-up service and an affiliate-transfer
   premium. They are shown, counted and disclosed now, never summed — the treatment reactive
   charges already had.
4. **"Ceiling" and "exceeds" were missing from the eligibility vocabulary.** I&M's Tariff G.S.
   states a **1,000 kW class ceiling**; neither word matched, so G.S. had no ceiling and a **300 MW
   load was quoted a schedule it is 300× too large for**. Measured: adding those two words
   reclassifies **exactly 1 of the 31** eligibility rows in the estate.
5. **The renderer kept a second, drifted copy of that same vocabulary.** It re-derived the ceiling
   from components with the old regex, so the exporter's fix would not have reached the page. It now
   reads `sch.max_kw` and searches components only for the prose that explains it.
6. **Inherited schedule legs were counted as riders.** A large-load framework rides on a parent
   (IP-LL on IP) and the exporter relabels the inherited rows so the reader can see their origin —
   which made the renderer bucket the parent's own demand and energy as RIDERS. IP-LL showed
   **ENERGY $0** on all four classes with **$153.89M** in its Riders column. The money was right and
   every column it sat in was wrong.
7. **A phantom third transmission class.** Duke's connection charge spans all transmission tiers at
   once; read as a class of its own it held nothing but $855.37/month — demand $0, energy $0, and a
   **0.77¢/kWh headline that led the schedule's range**.
8. **A grandfathered class was priced as an option.** Duke's `secondary (closed class)`. Once riders
   began costing correctly it surfaced as a row with $0 demand, $0 energy and $5.82M of riders — a
   service that does not exist, priced.

## 10. THE LEG GUARD NOW DISTINGUISHES *MISSING* FROM *ABSENT*

The guard — a row missing a whole billing leg refuses to show an effective rate — is what made all
of this findable and it stays. But it could not tell two different things apart, and was refusing
**AES PH** for telling the truth: Process Heating publishes two energy blocks, a customer charge,
and **no demand charge anywhere in the book**. A $0 demand column there is the tariff.

The exporter now states `has_demand_leg` / `has_energy_leg` per schedule, so a leg is "missing" only
when the schedule HAS one and this class did not get it. The guard still fires on NIPSCO **633**,
which does publish $24.72/kW-month, and comes off the schedules that never had one.

## 11. THE ±20% BAND — replaced with something defensible

The operator's point C was right: a flat band called correct answers failures. ⛔ The wrong fix
would have been a tolerance formula tuned until the numbers pass.

**What the two directions actually mean is asymmetric**, so the band is:

- **Below the average → suspicious.** Every low reading in this codebase's history was a defect:
  the absent rider stack read −36%, a dropped transmission charge −14%, a missing demand leg 0.26¢.
  Not one was a bargain.
- **Above the average → expected, up to a point.** A NEW large-load schedule prices at long-run
  marginal cost; the benchmark reflects decades of embedded cost.

So **−20% to +60%**, defined **once** in `market.html` and read by both the schedule verdict and the
per-row mark. And rather than invent a load-scaling formula, the page now carries the one published
fact that makes the comparison readable — EIA-861's own industrial customer count:

> *"That average covers 2,134 industrial customers using about 3,665 MWh a year each — you would use
> 609× that, so it is a sanity check on our METHOD, not a prediction of your price."*

## 12. PER-UTILITY INDEPENDENCE — proven, not asserted

The adapters existed. **Proving they isolate is a different claim**, and the operator was right to
press it: the arithmetic underneath is deliberately shared, which is how the previous session kept
trading one defect for another.

`scripts/tariff_fingerprint.py` hashes, per utility, every field that can change a bill — billing
leg, rate, months, class binding, block ladder, conditional/reactive status — and names exactly
which publishers moved. It is deliberately **blind to prose**, so rewording a `notes` string does
not read as a costing change.

⭐ **Demonstrated: perturbing ONLY Duke's `closed_classes` moved exactly 1 of 73 utilities, and
reverting restored the baseline byte-for-byte.** Run `export_tariffs.py` first, or you fingerprint a
stale build.

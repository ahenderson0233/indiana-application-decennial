Indiana statewide demand (FERC-714, monthly MWh)

This is the whole state's appetite, and it is the denominator your project lands in. A 300 MW campus at an 85% load factor draws about 2.2 TWh a year — put that against the trace below and you can see immediately whether you are a rounding error or a visible share of Indiana's load. It decides which conversation you are in: a visible share is what triggers a large-load tariff, a longer interconnection study, and political attention. Context for positioning, not a site test.

CEMS gross generation (monthly)

The chart above is what Indiana uses; this is what its emissions-monitored plants actually burn — the supply half of the same picture. Read the two together: generation falling while demand holds flat means retirements are outrunning replacement, and that is precisely what tightens the interconnection queue and lifts capacity prices in the years you would be energising. It changes your timing and your contingency plan, not your site choice. Context only.

Gas OAC — Indiana locations (Panhandle Eastern & Trunkline publish county)

This is the only gas number at a grain you can site against — a named delivery point, in a named county, with what the pipeline says is unsold today. The last column turns that into the generation it could actually support. Roughly 50 MMcf/d feeds a 300 MW plant, so a point posting a few thousand dekatherms a day is a boiler connection, not a data-centre power supply.
What to do with it: shortlist points whose spare capacity clears your load with margin, then confirm with the pipeline — posted capacity is a daily operational figure, not a reservation, and it can be sold to someone else tomorrow. Demand response — load the utilities can already shed

Two readings. It measures how much peak the utility can already remove without building anything, so a utility with deep demand response has more room for a new large load than its headline capacity suggests. It also signals whether an interruptible or curtailable tariff is likely to be offered to you — those trade a lower price for the utility's right to cut you, which suits a BESS far better than a data centre carrying an uptime commitment. Context for the negotiation, not a site test.

What would power cost here?

A yearly bill and an effective price per kWh for your load, built from the utility's own tariff — the rate schedule plus every rider that bills on it. Riders are not a separate topic; at I&M the stack alone is worth about +8.6 $/kW-month.
Every service voltage is priced side by side, because taking service at transmission rather than primary distribution is usually the largest controllable line on a large bill — a $9.30/kW-month spread at AES Indiana, roughly $3.9M a year on 35 MW. You should not need to know which voltage you want before you can see what the choice costs.

The rate engine — what we can defend, and what we refuse to quote

The URDB table above is flattened and is a cross-check only. This is the itemised engine: a wholesale floor from ISO settlement data, the tariff eligibility test, and an explicit list of the components no Indiana publisher has put in the public record.
Operator ruling, 2026-08-17: this is NOT a floor under an Indiana bundled rate, and it is no longer presented as one. The old framing was borrowed from a restructured market, where a retail supplier prices energy off the wholesale market and the utility bills regulated wires on top — there, wholesale really does flow through.
Indiana is vertically integrated. The utility owns its generation and recovers it through base rates set in an IURC rate case, so a bundled customer never buys at the market price. What actually passes fuel and purchased-power cost through is the Fuel Adjustment Clause, plus the RTO cost rider — and the utility’s embedded generation cost can sit either side of today’s market price, so a rate below LMP is not evidence of an error.
What it is still good for: the cost of energy if you buy it as energy — a PPA, a block purchase, or behind-the-meter generation — and as a read on regional energy cost. Treat it as an alternative procurement route, not as a bound on the tariff.
